2. Ask what makes the work meaningful and workable.

CHAPTER 13 / MOTIVATION IN THE WORKPLACE

NARRATION

Motivation concerns what energizes, directs, and sustains effort. Content theories ask which needs or features of work matter. Maslow's hierarchy draws attention to needs from basic security through belonging, esteem, and self-fulfillment. It is a model, not a reliable ladder for diagnosing each colleague. Herzberg distinguishes hygiene factors, such as working conditions and supervision, from motivators connected to achievement, recognition, responsibility, and the work itself. In that model, removing dissatisfaction does not automatically create satisfaction. That is not a reason to neglect pay or conditions. At Cedar Crossing, repairing an unreliable audio workstation removes a barrier. Giving an interested employee meaningful responsibility for shaping the listening experience offers a different kind of opportunity. Job enrichment adds meaningful scope and discretion, rather than simply more tasks. Ask what the person values and what resources the task requires. People can value stability, learning, relationships, or accomplishment differently, and their priorities can change. A theory helps frame questions; conversation and evidence help answer them.

Content theories in Chapter 13

Maslow distinguishes physiological, safety, social, esteem, and self-actualization needs. McClelland examines learned needs for achievement, affiliation, and power. These frameworks help organize questions, not label a person permanently.

Herzberg’s distinction

Hygiene or maintenance factors concern the work context: pay, security, working conditions, supervision, policy, and relationships. Motivators concern job content: achievement, recognition, the work itself, responsibility, and advancement. The textbook notes inconclusive research. Treat the distinction as a lens, not proof that money never matters or that all employees respond identically.

Job enrichment

Job enrichment increases meaningful responsibility and opportunities to use capabilities. More repetitive tasks alone are job enlargement, not necessarily enrichment.



3. Check every link between effort and a valued outcome.

CHAPTER 13 / MOTIVATION IN THE WORKPLACE

NARRATION

Process theories ask how people decide where to put effort. Vroom's expectancy theory connects three perceptions. Expectancy asks whether effort can produce the needed performance. Instrumentality asks whether that performance will lead to an outcome. Valence asks how much the person values that outcome. The model multiplies these factors, so one missing link can undermine the whole connection. Imagine an employee who wants to learn audio editing but has neither working software nor practice time. Promising more recognition does not repair the effort-to-performance link. Clear instructions, equipment, and supported practice might help. Even with those in place, a promised opportunity must be credible and valued. Equity theory adds a different question: how does someone judge their contributions and outcomes compared with a relevant other? Perceived unfairness can matter even when a manager believes the arrangement is reasonable. Investigate the comparison and the circumstances. Neither formula nor comparison lets us calculate a colleague's actual motivation from a distance.

Vroom’s model

Motivational force = expectancy × instrumentality × valence in the textbook model. Figure 13.6 clarifies expectancy as effort leading to performance, instrumentality as performance leading to reward, and valence as the reward’s value. This is a conceptual model of perceptions, not a measured score assigned to the fictional employee.

Equity

Equity theory concerns perceived relationships between inputs and outcomes and comparisons with others. The relevant comparison is not simply whether everyone receives the same amount. Investigate workload, contribution, opportunities, outcomes, and the employee’s understanding before assuming either fairness or unfairness.

Other process approaches

Reinforcement examines how consequences shape behavior. Goal-setting emphasizes the direction supplied by clear goals and feedback, with attention to commitment and capability. Positive reinforcement adds a desirable consequence; negative reinforcement removes an aversive condition and is not the same as punishment. Consider whether a reward encourages the intended behavior or an unintended shortcut.

4. Match leadership to the work and situation.

CHAPTER 14 / ORGANIZATIONAL LEADERSHIP

NARRATION

Leadership influences people toward a purpose. Formal authority can help, but a title alone does not create trust or good judgment. Contingency theories examine the fit among leader, people, task, and setting. Fiedler's model treats leadership style as relatively fixed and examines leader-member relations, task structure, and position power. Its central question is how to match style and situation. House's path-goal theory instead allows leaders to vary their behavior. Directive behavior clarifies the work. Supportive behavior attends to people's needs. Participative behavior involves people in decisions. Achievement-oriented behavior sets challenging goals with confidence and support. For Cedar Crossing's experienced collections specialist, detailed instructions for every familiar step may add little. A colleague new to audio editing may need a clear demonstration and an accessible way to ask questions. The choice depends on the task and person, not a stereotype about age or job title. Explain the purpose, notice what support is needed, and revise the approach as conditions change.

Fiedler’s situational variables

Leader-member relations concern trust and acceptance. Task structure concerns clarity and structure of the work. Position power concerns formal authority. The textbook presents task-oriented leadership as fitting the most and least favorable conditions and relationship-oriented leadership as fitting intermediate conditions. Do not turn this into a universal causal law or describe Fiedler as simply switching style at will.

Path-goal theory

Directive, supportive, participative, and achievement-oriented behaviors can vary with employee characteristics and the work environment. Clarifying a path can include explaining goals, removing obstacles, and connecting performance with appropriate outcomes. Participation is one possible behavior, not the automatic choice for every decision.

Styles and evidence

Autocratic, democratic, and laissez-faire labels describe broad patterns, not sufficient diagnoses. Trait approaches examine leader characteristics; behavioral approaches examine what leaders do. Evaluate the actual actions, context, and effects rather than assuming one label guarantees effectiveness.



5. Share leadership while keeping responsibility clear.

CHAPTER 14 / ORGANIZATIONAL LEADERSHIP

NARRATION

A heroic image puts the leader at the center of every success. Shared or distributed leadership recognizes that influence and expertise can come from several people. At Cedar Crossing, a colleague with audio experience might guide a technical decision while another leads discussion of local sources. Sharing leadership still requires clear authority and support. Transactional leadership emphasizes exchanges and agreed performance; transformational leadership emphasizes shared purpose, development, and change. They are not simply bad and good alternatives. Collins's Level Five leadership combines personal humility with professional will, directing ambition toward the organization rather than personal acclaim. Treat that research-based description as a lens, not a promise that one personality guarantees success. Also attend to harmful behavior. Repeated humiliation, intimidation, or suppression of concerns can damage trust and information sharing. Toxic leadership concerns patterns and effects; a disagreement alone does not establish it. Invite informed challenge, give credit accurately, and make it possible to raise concerns through appropriate channels.

Shared influence and formal responsibility

Distributed leadership is not the disappearance of managers. Different people can lead particular contributions while the institution retains decision boundaries, accountability, and support. Clear coordination helps prevent overlapping or abandoned responsibilities.

Transactional and transformational leadership

Transactional approaches connect performance and exchanges. Transformational approaches develop commitment to purpose and change. These perspectives can overlap in practice; none eliminates the need to examine ethical conduct and results.

Level Five and toxic behavior

The textbook’s prose describes Collins’s Level Five leadership as personal humility plus professional will. It does not mean passivity or weak standards. Toxic leadership involves harmful patterns such as abuse of power or intimidation; discuss specific evidence and effects rather than diagnosing a personality or using the term for every unpopular decision.



6. Examine benefits, rights, and fairness together.

CHAPTER 15 / PROFESSIONAL ETHICS

NARRATION

An attractive outcome can still raise ethical questions. Suppose a donated photograph would enrich Cedar Crossing's display, but its description reveals personal information about someone pictured. First establish the facts, the relevant permissions, and who could be affected. Utilitarian reasoning compares benefits and harms across possible actions. An individual-rights approach asks whose dignity, privacy, or other rights must be respected. Social justice asks whether the treatment and distribution of benefits and burdens are fair. These lenses can point in different directions. A popular exhibit does not settle every concern. Martha Smith's model adds four loyalties: self, clients or patrons, the profession, and the employing institution. A manager may have to negotiate tensions among them. Professional codes, including the American Library Association's Code of Ethics, offer principles rather than a ready answer for every situation. Consider alternatives, explain the reasoning, and seek appropriate guidance when needed. Ethical judgment and legal compliance are related, but they are not identical questions.

7. A sent message is only part of communication.

CHAPTER 16 / ORGANIZATIONAL COMMUNICATION

NARRATION

Communication involves creating and interpreting meaning. A sender encodes a message, uses a channel, and a receiver decodes it. Feedback helps test what was understood. Noise is anything that hinders the process, not only sound. Jargon, assumptions, interruptions, or an inaccessible format can interfere. At Cedar Crossing, the instruction make the captions accessible sounds clear until colleagues discover they mean different things by accessible. A shared example and a check-back can reveal the difference. Downward communication moves from supervisors to staff; upward communication carries information and concerns toward supervisors. Horizontal communication connects colleagues at the same organizational level. Informal networks also carry useful information, but important decisions still need reliable confirmation. Choose a channel suited to the task. A written specification may support reference later; a conversation may help resolve ambiguity. Listen, ask for clarification, and confirm the agreed meaning. Sending another copy of the same unclear message may simply repeat the original problem.

Flow directions

Downward: supervisor to staff, including direction and information. Upward: staff to supervisor, including feedback, concerns, and suggestions. Horizontal: exchange among colleagues at the same level. The assigned text also groups some lateral or diagonal exchanges under horizontal communication; actual diagonal exchanges may cross both unit and hierarchical boundaries. Barriers and active listening

People may filter information, interpret words differently, miss nonverbal cues, or face overload, status barriers, and inaccessible channels. Active listening involves attention, clarification, and checking interpretation. Agreement is not required to understand someone’s message accurately.

Formal and informal channels

Formal channels follow recognized arrangements; informal communication arises through relationships. Informal information may be timely but still needs verification. An official channel can also carry ambiguity or error. Preserve an accessible shared record of important decisions without assuming that one email guarantees understanding.

Five conflict responses

Competing emphasizes one’s own position; accommodating yields to another; avoiding postpones or withdraws; compromising exchanges concessions; collaborating explores an approach that addresses important interests. These are responses to situations, not permanent personality labels.

9. Give participation a clear scope and real support.

CHAPTER 17 / EMPOWERING EMPLOYEES THROUGH THE USE OF TEAMS

NARRATION

Empowerment expands people's ability to influence and carry out their work. Participation invites input into decisions. Neither works well when the invitation hides fixed decisions or leaves people without information and resources. Cedar Crossing's project group can propose the display sequence within agreed space and access requirements. The manager explains which choices the group may make, which need approval, and where to get help. The group also needs relevant skills, usable information, time, and a clear result. A small decision agreement can identify purpose, participants, decision authority, process, and follow-up. Different decisions may call for consultation, delegated choice, voting, or consensus. Define the process before relying on it; a vote and consensus are not interchangeable. Self-management means taking on management responsibilities within an agreed scope. It does not mean that support and accountability disappear. Evaluate whether participation improves both the work and people's ability to contribute, and change arrangements when the evidence shows a problem.

10. Build team conditions and review how they work.

CHAPTER 17 / EMPOWERING EMPLOYEES THROUGH THE USE OF TEAMS

NARRATION

Effective teams need relevant skills, mutual trust, appropriate size, good communication, appropriate leadership, clear goals, and loyalty to the shared work. Loyalty does not require silence about a weak idea. Tuckman's model describes forming, storming, norming, performing, and adjourning. Forming establishes purpose and relationships. Storming involves challenges over direction and control. Norming develops shared expectations. Performing focuses energy on the task. Adjourning concerns closure when a temporary team's work ends. These stages offer a way to interpret patterns; they do not give every team a fixed timetable or guarantee that problems resolve without action. Watch for social loafing, when individuals reduce effort in a group, and groupthink, when pressure for agreement crowds out critical evaluation. At Cedar Crossing, the temporary display group needs clear contributions and room for informed disagreement. Contingency management brings the lesson together: examine the task, people, and setting, choose an approach, and review its effects on staff and service.

The five-stage model

Forming concerns purpose, structure, and relationships. Storming involves questions about direction and control. Norming develops shared expectations and identity. Performing focuses collective energy on accomplishing the work. Adjourning concerns closure for a time-limited team. Name a stage only when evidence supports the interpretation; different patterns may coexist. The textbook cites Tuckman and Jensen’s 1977 extension for the five-stage model.

Seven characteristics in the chapter prose

Relevant skills; mutual trust; appropriate size; good communication; appropriate leadership; clear goals; loyalty. The chapter’s figure omits leadership, but its prose includes it. No single team size guarantees success. Leadership may come from team members, with managers coaching or facilitating as appropriate.

Group dynamics and contingency

Social loafing concerns reduced individual effort in group work; groupthink concerns conformity displacing critical evaluation. Risky shift describes a tendency discussed in the text for some groups to choose more risk than individuals would, not a universal law for all teams. General contingency management integrates the situation across management functions; it is broader than Fiedler’s specific leadership model.

Library Shift is optional and ungraded. It contains one practice decision and eight scored decisions, each with three responses and explanatory feedback. Untimed decisions are selected by default. Timed challenge mode allows 40 seconds for practice and 30 seconds for each scored decision. It does not speed up. Every answer pauses for feedback, and Continue starts the next decision. Pause stops the clock; Resume continues. Leaving the game or hiding the page pauses an active turn. Restart resets the current shift. Skip game opens course next steps. Use the response buttons or the 1, 2, and 3 keys while focus is inside the play area; shortcuts can be switched off. Sound starts off and has its own toggle. Reduced-motion settings are available. No essential information depends on sound or motion.

Start with three lives. Practice affects neither points nor lives. A correct scored response earns 100 points plus a streak bonus of 20 points for each additional consecutive correct response, capped at 100 bonus points. Bonus decisions 3 and 6 double those points and add one life, up to five. An incorrect response or expired timer uses one life and resets the streak. With no lives left, the shift ends; all feedback remains available here. A fully correct shift earns 1,640 points. Separate timed and untimed best scores are stored only in this browser; if storage is blocked, they last only for this visit. Scores never go to Canvas or a shared leaderboard.



Practice decision

A colleague asks what the display should help visitors understand. What is the useful first move?

1. Explain the shared purpose.

Feedback: Yes. A clear purpose helps people connect their contributions.

2. List every possible task.

Feedback: Tasks matter, but this question asks why the work is being done.

3. Ask them to guess.

Feedback: A shared purpose needs explanation, not guesswork.

Strongest response: Explain the shared purpose.



Decision 1

The audio tool fails repeatedly. Staff are frustrated. What addresses the barrier described?

1. Offer praise alone.

Feedback: Recognition does not repair an unusable tool.

2. Repair the tool and check usability.

Feedback: Yes. Workable conditions provide a foundation; meaningful work and recognition remain distinct concerns.

3. Add more editing tasks.

Feedback: More tasks would add load without addressing the equipment problem.

Strongest response: Repair the tool and check usability.



Decision 2

An experienced colleague knows the task, but a new colleague needs guidance. What fits path-goal thinking?

1. Give both no guidance.

Feedback: New-to-task staff may need clarification and support.

2. Use identical instructions forever.

Feedback: Identical instructions may not address different needs.

3. Adapt support to the task and person.

Feedback: Yes. Path-goal theory allows different behaviors as needs and conditions change.

Strongest response: Adapt support to the task and person.



Decision 3 · bonus round

An exhibit choice benefits visitors but may expose personal information. What should the group examine?

1. Benefits, rights, and fairness.

Feedback: Yes. Consider competing values, missing facts, and affected people.

2. Popularity alone.

Feedback: Popularity is one possible benefit, not a complete ethical analysis.

3. Only the manager’s preference.

Feedback: A personal preference does not substitute for ethical reasoning.

Strongest response: Benefits, rights, and fairness.



Decision 4

Two colleagues interpret the same instruction differently. What helps identify the misunderstanding?

1. Send it again unchanged.

Feedback: Repeating ambiguity may repeat the problem.

2. Treat silence as agreement.

Feedback: Silence does not establish shared understanding.

3. Ask for a check-back with an example.

Feedback: Yes. Feedback reveals how the message was interpreted.

Strongest response: Ask for a check-back with an example.



Decision 5

A group avoids examining a weak idea because nobody wants disagreement. What should a facilitator encourage?

1. Immediate agreement.

Feedback: Quick agreement can conceal important information.

2. Respectful critical evaluation.

Feedback: Yes. Critical evaluation helps counter groupthink while preserving respect.

3. Silence from uncertain members.

Feedback: Silencing uncertainty can strengthen pressure to conform.

Strongest response: Respectful critical evaluation.



Decision 6 · bonus round

The group has a decision to make but no access to necessary information. What supports empowerment?

1. Provide information and clarify authority.

Feedback: Yes. Meaningful discretion needs information, boundaries, and practical support.

2. Announce that support is over.

Feedback: Withdrawing help does not make a group capable of deciding.

3. Keep the decision rules hidden.

Feedback: Hidden rules weaken meaningful influence.

Strongest response: Provide information and clarify authority.



Decision 7

A temporary project ends. What makes its closure useful?

1. Skip discussion of the work.

Feedback: Closure can preserve learning and clarify responsibilities.

2. Review learning and arrange handoffs.

Feedback: Yes. Adjourning includes attention to closure; a review and handoff can support future work.

3. Assume everyone knows what happens next.

Feedback: Unstated assumptions can leave work and learning unresolved.

Strongest response: Review learning and arrange handoffs.



Decision 8

A manager labels a group “storming.” What must still happen?

1. Wait without reviewing service.

Feedback: A stage label does not justify neglecting service problems.

2. Treat the label as a full diagnosis.

Feedback: A label organizes interpretation but does not settle causes or responses.

3. Check evidence and needed support.

Feedback: Yes. Examine the actual conditions, effects, and support needed.

Strongest response: Check evidence and needed support.
